SEO engine

It works out which blogs to write. You confirm every claim before one goes live.

What does it actually do?

Seven steps, from reading your site to measuring what a post did.

  1. It studies your site

    Up to 1,000 pages, found from your sitemap first and links second, obeying your robots.txt.

  2. It finds your competitors

    From your Marketing engine, from a web search, and from sites that rank for two or more of your own searches. You keep or remove each one.

  3. It builds a topic map

    The keywords they rank for and you do not, grouped into a pillar page and the pages that support it.

  4. It recommends blogs

    From that map, from rising searches, and from what Marketing and News ask for.

  5. It drafts from your material

    Nothing starts without a piece of original evidence from you.

  6. You confirm the claims, then publish

    As a GitHub pull request or a WordPress draft.

  7. It checks and measures

    A live check, the sitemap sent to Search Console, then search numbers at day 3, 7, 14 and 30.

What does the site study look at?

Every page it can reach, opened in a real browser. It also records what a crawler that runs no JavaScript receives, because some AI crawlers read only the raw HTML.

  • Title, description, headings, word count and links for each page.
  • Orphans: pages nothing else on the site links to.
  • Whether each page is indexed, checked with Google's URL Inspection and with Bing.

How does it pick my competitors?

It suggests them, and you keep or remove each one. A competitor you approved in the Marketing engine arrives marked as recommended, and is read first. It is never kept on Marketing's say-so alone.

  • A site from search results only counts if it shows up for at least two of your searches. Wikipedia, YouTube, Reddit, review sites and the like are left out.
  • It finds each competitor's blog from its feed, its sitemap or its blog page, and reads their newest posts.
  • Remove a competitor and it drops out of your keywords and topics straight away.

From a comment in the code:

“Competitors' traffic is never shown: it lives in their Search Console.”

Where do the recommended blogs come from?

From gaps against your competitors, from searches that are rising, and from the other engines. Every Marketing campaign asks for a blog, and so does every News topic whose searches are rising.

  • They sit in their own list, Requested by Marketing and News. Once drafted they stay in the list, labelled, so you can see the request was answered.
  • Demand is shown as Bing's weekly searches when Bing reports them, and otherwise as how many Google suggestions extend the phrase. The two are never mixed.
  • If Google blocks the check with a captcha, it says so rather than reporting you as unranked, and your previous topic map is kept.
  • If one of your pages already ranks for the keyword, it suggests refreshing that page rather than writing a second one to compete with it.

Will it fill my blog with generic AI text?

It will not draft at all until you give it something only you have: a customer insight, internal data, a workflow, a screenshot, a benchmark, a case study, an expert quote or a first-hand opinion.

  • Every figure in the draft has to appear in the source it cites.
  • A link that goes neither to your site nor to one of the given sources is removed.
  • Anything that still fails the checks is listed for you to review, not hidden.

What stops it saying something untrue about my product?

You do, and the code makes you. Every sentence that names your brand, every list item, every FAQ answer and the image are listed as claims. You tick each one, and Publish stays disabled until all are confirmed.

  • Editing a claim after you confirmed it cancels the confirmation, and so does changing the image.
  • The check runs again on the publishing side, so a draft with unconfirmed claims waits rather than going out.

One limit worth knowing

Claims are found by looking for your brand name. A sentence that describes your product without naming it is not listed, so read the whole draft, not only the ticks.

Where does it publish?

To a GitHub repository or a WordPress site, using a connection that belongs to that one site.

  • GitHub: one commit on a new seo/ branch and a pull request. It never writes your default branch. Pressing Publish merges that pull request; if branch protection or a conflict refuses the merge, the pull request stays open for a person.
  • WordPress: the post is created as a draft. It goes live when you publish it in WordPress.
  • The post comes with its blog index entry, its sitemap entry, and links from up to three related pages, added only inside a paragraph that already discusses the topic.

What happens after it goes live?

A live check, the sitemap sent to Google Search Console, then measurement at day 3, 7, 14 and 30, and monthly after that.

  • The live check confirms the page is served, the article text is in the HTML before any script runs, and the title, canonical link and structured data survived your site's build.
  • From Search Console: impressions, clicks, click-through rate and average position. Missing data is shown as unmeasured, with the reason.
  • A button sends your sitemap to Google and Bing, and asks Bing to crawl pages it has not yet.
  • Every week it compares two 28-day windows and flags pages that are slipping.

Does it watch my competitors?

Once you start the competitor monitor, it reads the blogs of up to eight kept competitors daily for new posts, and rechecks where each new post ranks weekly for 30 days.

When a post ranks above its own site's usual position, you get an alert in the app's inbox. That is the topic working for them right now, and you can draft on the keyword from there.

Can I stop it running on its own?

Yes. The Engine on/off switch pauses its hourly run: the competitor monitor, the pull request and WordPress follow-up, and the measurements. Buttons you press inside the engine still work.

What does it cost?

Each finished job is 1 task. Competitor jobs (gaps, authority, rich results, monitor) are 1 task per kept competitor. Finding competitors is free. Keeping a found competitor is 1 task; one recommended from Marketing is free. Blog images are paid from media credits.

Questions

Does it need Google Search Console?

It works without it, but measurement, the index check and the refresh suggestions read from it. Without it those numbers show as unmeasured rather than guessed.

Where do the images come from?

Your own site's images first, then Wikimedia Commons under licences that allow reuse, with the credit attached. An AI image is made only when you ask, and is paid from media credits.

Does it measure backlinks or domain authority?

No. It does not measure either, and it does not score keyword density or a word count target.

Does it run on Android?

The site study, competitor reading and rank checks need the desktop app's browser, so run those on Windows or Linux.
