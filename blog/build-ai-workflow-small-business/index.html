How to build an AI workflow for a small business

By Workforce 2.0 team · September 30, 2026

The useful first AI workflow is rarely the biggest one. Pick one job that repeats, decide what a good result means, and keep a person at the point where a mistake would matter.

Share this guideLinkedInXFacebook

Start with a job, not an AI tool

“Use AI for marketing” is too broad to build or measure. A better starting point is a job with a beginning, an end and a clear owner: prepare a weekly pipeline review, turn a customer call into follow-up tasks, research a shortlist of leads, or draft three campaign ideas for approval.

Write the current sequence down in ordinary language. Include what triggers it, the inputs it needs, the output someone expects and the point where it leaves the company. That becomes the workflow. In Workforce, a custom engine starts from those written steps rather than a blank technical diagram. See how custom engines are built.

Choose a result you can inspect

Good automation has a visible outcome. For a sales workflow that might be a verified lead list and draft outreach. For marketing it may be a campaign plan, calendar and draft posts. For operations it could be a set of assigned tasks with the source notes attached.

Avoid using “save time” as the only measure. Instead, record one quality measure as well: verified contact details, approved posts, follow-ups completed, or leads per campaign. The marketing engine, for example, reports back on leads per post rather than treating reach as proof of value.

Put approval at the edge of the workflow

Reading, sorting and drafting are usually low-risk. Sending an email, publishing a post, changing a customer record or spending money are different: they affect someone outside the business. Put the approval step immediately before that outward action.

This is how Workforce is designed: the planner can coordinate work, while workers are limited to the apps and permissions you allow. You can read more about the model on the platform page and the practical controls on the data and permissions page.

Connect only the tools the job needs

Do not begin by connecting every account. A useful first workflow needs only the source of information and the place where its result belongs. For example, a meeting follow-up flow might need one calendar, one inbox and a task list. A campaign workflow may need your brand material, the channels you approve, and a way to count inquiries.

Restricting the first version keeps the review simple and makes it obvious where a bad result came from. Add another connection only when it removes a real hand-off. The integrations page shows the services Workforce can connect when that next step is justified.

Run one cycle, then improve the weak step

The first run is evidence, not a finished system. Compare its result with the work you would have done manually. Did it use the right source? Did the draft need the same correction every time? Did the approval arrive with enough context to make a quick decision?

Improve the step that failed instead of adding more automation around it. If research is weak, clarify the sources. If the draft misses your voice, add examples of work you would sign your name to. If approvals are slow, make the decision and supporting evidence visible together.

A simple first workflow to try

  1. Pick one recurring job. Choose something that happens at least weekly and has a clear result.
  2. Describe the steps. Use plain language: read this, compare it with that, prepare this output, ask before sending.
  3. Set the approval point. Keep it directly before anything external or irreversible.
  4. Connect the smallest useful set of tools. Start with the systems that supply the input and receive the output.
  5. Review one completed cycle. Keep what worked and fix the one weakest step before expanding it.
